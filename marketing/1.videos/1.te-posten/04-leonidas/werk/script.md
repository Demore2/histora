## Teaser
What if you were Leonidas?
At seven, you'd be taken from your mother and starved, on purpose.
You'd become king by accident.
You'd march against the biggest army the world had ever seen, with three hundred men.
And you'd know you were never coming home.
So how does a third son nobody wanted on the throne end up in the most famous last stand in history?

## I · Born in Sparta
You're born around 540 BC, in Sparta, in the south of Greece.
Your father is a king, because Sparta has two kings at the same time.
But you're not his first son.
Your father's first wife couldn't have children, so the elders forced him to take a second wife.
She gives him a son, Cleomenes.
Then, suddenly, the first wife has three sons of her own.
You're the second of them.
So there are two brothers ahead of you, and nobody expects you to wear a crown.
In Sparta, even a baby has to pass a test.
According to Plutarch, the elders look at every newborn, and a weak one is left on the mountain.
You pass.

## II · The agoge
At seven, you're taken from your mother and sent to the agoge, Sparta's school for boys.
Your hair is cut short, and you walk barefoot, summer and winter.
You get one cloak a year, and you sleep on reeds you pick yourself, by hand, from the river.
They feed you too little, on purpose, so you learn to steal food.
If you're caught, you're beaten.
Not for stealing, but for getting caught.
One story tells of a boy who hid a stolen fox under his cloak.
The fox bit into him, and he said nothing, until he fell down dead.
You also learn to speak in as few words as possible.
Sparta's region is called Laconia, and that's where our word laconic comes from.
At twenty, you become a soldier.
At thirty, you may finally live in your own house.
And for the rest of your life, you eat in a mess hall with fifteen other men, every single night.

## III · King by accident
Your half-brother Cleomenes becomes king.
Your older brother Dorieus is so angry that he leaves Sparta forever, and dies in a war far away, in Sicily.
Cleomenes rules for almost thirty years, but in the end he loses his mind.
His family puts him in chains, and soon he's dead.
He leaves no son, only a daughter.
So in 490 BC, the crown lands on your head.
You're about fifty, and you never trained to be king.
You marry Cleomenes' daughter, Gorgo, your own niece.
And Gorgo is not a quiet queen.
As a girl of eight, she had warned her father not to take a foreigner's bribe, and he listened.
A foreign woman once asked her why Spartan women were the only ones who ruled their men.
Gorgo answered: because we're the only ones who give birth to men.

## IV · Earth and water
To the east lies the Persian Empire, the largest empire the world has ever seen.
Its king, Darius, sends messengers to every Greek city.
They ask for earth and water, a sign that you surrender.
In Athens, the messengers are thrown into a pit.
In Sparta, they're thrown down a well.
Dig it out yourselves, the Spartans tell them.
Darius dies before he can take revenge.
But his son Xerxes doesn't forget.
For four years he prepares the biggest invasion of the ancient world.
He builds two bridges of boats across the sea, more than a kilometre long.
When a storm destroys them, he orders the sea to be whipped three hundred times.
Then he builds them again, and his army crosses for seven days and seven nights.

## V · The oracle
Herodotus says Xerxes brought more than two and a half million men.
Historians today think it was more like one or two hundred thousand.
That's still one of the biggest armies the world had ever seen.
Sparta asks the oracle at Delphi what will happen.
The answer is terrible: either Sparta will fall, or Sparta will mourn a dead king.
You're that king.
But Sparta is celebrating a sacred festival, and the army isn't allowed to march.
So you go ahead, with a small guard of three hundred men.
You choose only men who already have sons, so that no family line dies out.
On the way, other Greek cities join you, until you have about seven thousand men.
That's the part the movies leave out.

## VI · The hot gates
You march to Thermopylae, the hot gates, named after its hot springs.
On one side are steep mountains, and on the other side is the sea.
In one place the road is so narrow that only one wagon can pass.
There, you rebuild an old stone wall, and wait.
Xerxes sends a rider to look at your camp.
He sees Spartans exercising, and calmly combing their long hair.
Xerxes laughs, but an exiled Spartan king in his tent warns him.
When Spartans are about to risk their lives, he says, they do their hair.
Xerxes waits four days, sure that you'll run.
Then he sends you a message: hand over your weapons.
Your answer is two words: come and take them.

## VII · Fighting in the shade
On the fifth day, the attack begins.
Wave after wave runs into your shield wall, and breaks.
Your men pretend to flee, then turn around and cut down the soldiers chasing them.
A Spartan named Dienekes is told that the Persian arrows will block out the sun.
Good, he answers, then we'll fight in the shade.
Xerxes sends in his best troops, the ten thousand Immortals.
They fail too.
Three times, Herodotus says, Xerxes jumps up from his throne in fear for his army.
The second day goes the same way.
For a moment, it looks like the impossible might happen.

## VIII · The goat path
Then a local man named Ephialtes comes to Xerxes' tent.
For a reward, he shows the Persians a goat path through the mountains, around the pass.
That night, the Immortals climb it in the dark.
At dawn, your lookouts run down the mountain: you're surrounded.
You know what the oracle said.
You send most of the Greeks home, to fight another day.
You stay, with your three hundred, and seven hundred men from Thespiae who refuse to leave.
According to later writers, you tell your men: eat a good breakfast, for tonight we dine in the underworld.
This time you don't wait behind the wall.
You march out into the open, and fight.
Somewhere in that fight, you fall.
Your men fight over your body, and drag it back four times.
When their spears break, they fight with swords.
When their swords break, Herodotus says, they fight with their hands and their teeth.
And then it's over.

## IX · Go tell the Spartans
Xerxes takes the pass, and he burns Athens.
But he has lost about twenty thousand men, among them two of his own brothers.
And the Greeks have seen that the Persians can be stopped.
A few weeks later, the Greek fleet crushes the Persian navy at Salamis.
The next summer, your nephew leads the Greeks to victory at Plataea.
Persia never invades Greece again.
At Thermopylae, the Greeks set up a stone with a short poem.
Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here, obeying their laws, we lie.
Forty years later, your bones are brought home to Sparta.
And today, on a bronze statue of you at the pass, are two Greek words.
Molon labe: come and take them.
