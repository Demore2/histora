## Teaser
What if you were Julius Caesar?
You'd be kidnapped by pirates, and laugh at them.
You'd conquer a land bigger than Italy.
You'd cross a small river and start a civil war.
And you'd be stabbed twenty-three times by your own friends.
So how does a broke boy from the backstreets of Rome get there?

## I · The boy from the Subura
You're born in July, in the year 100 BC, in Rome.
Your name is Gaius Julius Caesar.
Your family says it descends from the goddess Venus.
But your family has no money, and no real power.
You grow up in the Subura, a loud and dirty part of the city, full of shops, smoke and crowded apartment blocks.
Rome rules the Mediterranean, but it is tearing itself apart.
Generals fight each other with private armies, and politicians are murdered in the street.
You're about fifteen when your father dies suddenly, one morning, while putting on his shoes.
Now you're the head of the family.

## II · Hunted by a dictator
At eighteen, you're already married, to Cornelia, the daughter of a powerful politician.
Then a general named Sulla takes Rome and makes himself dictator.
He hunts down his enemies, and your new family is on the wrong side.
Sulla gives you an order: divorce your wife.
You say no.
So you lose your money, and your name goes on a list of people to be found.
You hide in the hills, sick with fever, sleeping in a different house every night.
Your mother's family begs Sulla to spare you, and in the end he gives in.
But he warns them: in this boy, I see many Mariuses.
Marius was the man Sulla hated most.

## III · Kidnapped by pirates
At twenty-five, you sail to Rhodes to study public speaking.
On the way, pirates capture your ship.
They want twenty talents of silver for your release.
You laugh, and tell them you're worth at least fifty.
For thirty-eight days you live among them like their leader.
You order them to be quiet when you want to sleep.
You read them your poems, and call them savages when they don't clap.
And you promise, smiling, that one day you'll come back and kill them all.
They think it's a joke.
When the ransom arrives, you're set free.
You raise a small fleet, you hunt the pirates down, and you capture them.
And you keep your promise.

## IV · Deep in debt
In Spain, you see a statue of Alexander the Great, and you start to cry.
Alexander had conquered the known world by your age, and you've done nothing.
So you go all in.
You borrow huge sums and spend them on games, feasts and gladiators for the people of Rome.
The people love you.
You run for chief priest of Rome against two older, richer men.
That morning you tell your mother: today you'll see me either as chief priest, or as an exile.
You win.
Then you make a secret deal with Rome's greatest general, Pompey, and its richest man, Crassus.
Together, the three of you run Rome from behind the scenes.
To seal the deal, Pompey marries your daughter, Julia.
And in 59 BC, you become consul, the highest office in Rome.

## V · Gaul
Next, you take command of the provinces that border Gaul, roughly modern France and Belgium.
It's a land of proud and warlike tribes.
Over the next eight years, you conquer all of it.
You build a bridge across the Rhine in just ten days, march into Germania, and then tear the bridge down behind you.
You become the first Roman general to land in Britain.
In 52 BC, the Gauls unite behind one leader, Vercingetorix.
You trap him in the hill fort of Alesia, and around it you build a ring of walls, eighteen kilometres long.
Then a huge Gallic army arrives to rescue him, so you build a second wall, facing outward.
You fight in the middle, between two armies.
And you win.
Vercingetorix rides out and lays his weapons at your feet.
But the price is terrible.
Ancient writers say a million Gauls were killed, and another million enslaved.
Historians think those numbers are too high, but whole peoples were destroyed.
And you send the writing home: a book about your own war, starring you.

## VI · The Rubicon
While you're away, everything falls apart.
Your daughter Julia dies, and Crassus is killed in a war in the East.
Now only you and Pompey are left, and Pompey has chosen the Senate's side.
The Senate orders you to give up your army and come home as a private man.
Without your army, your enemies will put you on trial and end your career.
In January of 49 BC, you stand at a small river called the Rubicon, the border of Italy.
No general may cross it with an army.
You hesitate.
Then you say: let the die be cast.
And you cross.
Pompey and half the Senate flee from Rome.
A year later, you crush Pompey's army at Pharsalus in Greece.
Pompey escapes to Egypt.
When you arrive, the Egyptians hand you his head.
And you weep.

## VII · Cleopatra
Egypt is in a civil war of its own, between a young king and his sister, Cleopatra.
Cleopatra is not allowed into the palace.
So, according to the story, she has herself carried in, rolled up in a bedding sack.
You're fifty-two, she's about twenty-one.
You take her side, and for months you are trapped in Alexandria, fighting the king's army in the streets.
Part of the city burns, and with it, books from the great library.
You win, and Cleopatra becomes queen.
Soon she has a son, and she names him Caesarion, little Caesar.
Then you march into Asia and beat a king in five days.
Your report to Rome is just three words: I came, I saw, I conquered.

## VIII · Dictator
By 45 BC, nobody is left to fight you.
Rome gives you everything: a golden chair, your face on coins, a month named after you.
That month is still called July.
You fix the calendar, which had drifted months away from the seasons.
To catch up, one year has to last four hundred and forty-five days.
Your calendar, with a leap day every four years, is used for more than sixteen hundred years.
You forgive many of your old enemies, and give some of them jobs.
One of them is a young senator named Brutus.
Then, in February of 44 BC, you are named dictator for life.
To many senators, that means one thing.
You are a king, and Rome hates kings.

## IX · The Ides of March
A fortune teller warns you: beware the fifteenth of March, the Ides.
The night before, your wife Calpurnia dreams that you die in her arms.
She begs you to stay home.
But you go to the Senate anyway.
On the way you pass the fortune teller and joke: the Ides of March have come.
He answers: yes, but they have not gone.
Inside, a group of senators surrounds you.
More than sixty men are in on the plot.
The first knife strikes your neck.
You fight back, until you see Brutus among them.
Then you pull your toga over your face.
You fall at the foot of a statue of Pompey.
Twenty-three wounds, and only one was deadly.
They believed they were saving the Republic.
Instead, they destroyed it.
Your will names your great-nephew, Octavian, as your son.
He's eighteen.
He becomes Augustus, Rome's first emperor.
And your name becomes a word for ruler: Kaiser, Tsar.
