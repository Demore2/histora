## Teaser
What if you were Saladin?
You'd start as a soldier who never wanted to go to war.
You'd destroy a crusader army on a burning hillside.
You'd take Jerusalem, and spare the people inside.
And you'd die with just one gold coin to your name.
So how does a Kurdish boy from Tikrit get there?

## A boy from Tikrit
His real name was Yusuf ibn Ayyub.
He was born around 1137 in Tikrit, a fortress town on the Tigris River, in what is now Iraq.
His family were Kurdish soldiers, and his father was governor of the fortress.
According to an old story, they had to flee Tikrit on the very night he was born.
His uncle Shirkuh had killed a man in a quarrel.
The family found work with Zengi, a powerful Turkish lord in northern Syria, and later with his son, Nur ad-Din.
Yusuf grew up in Damascus, one of the great cities of the Islamic world.
As a young man, he was said to care more about religion and books than about war.
The world around him was deeply divided.
In 1099, the knights of the First Crusade had taken Jerusalem and slaughtered many of the people inside.
They had built four Christian states along the coast.
And the Muslim world was split between rival princes, and between two rival caliphs, one in Baghdad and one in Cairo.
Nobody expected the quiet boy from Damascus to change any of that.

## Master of Egypt
Egypt was rich, but weak.
The crusaders wanted it, and so did Nur ad-Din.
In the 1160s he sent Shirkuh to Egypt three times, and young Yusuf had to go with his uncle.
He later said he went like a man being led to his death.
In 1169 Shirkuh became vizier, the most powerful minister in Egypt.
Two months later, Shirkuh was dead.
To everyone's surprise, the caliph chose the nephew to replace him.
He was about thirty-one.
The courtiers in Cairo probably thought he would be easy to control.
They were wrong.
He crushed a revolt of the palace guards and took firm control of the country.
In 1171, as the last Fatimid caliph lay dying, Saladin had the Friday prayers said in the name of the caliph in Baghdad.
Two hundred years of Fatimid rule ended without a battle.
He began building a great citadel on a hill above Cairo.
Egypt's rulers would govern from it for nearly seven hundred years.
On paper he was still Nur ad-Din's servant.
But Nur ad-Din was starting to suspect that his general wanted Egypt for himself.

## A leper king
In 1174, Nur ad-Din died, leaving an eleven-year-old son.
Saladin rode to Damascus and took the city.
Over the next twelve years he fought, negotiated and married his way to power over Aleppo, Mosul and the rest of Syria.
His enemies said he spent more time fighting other Muslims than fighting the crusaders.
There was some truth in that.
But slowly, his lands closed around the crusader states like a fist.
He did not always win.
In 1177 he invaded the Kingdom of Jerusalem.
Its king, Baldwin the Fourth, was sixteen years old, and he had leprosy.
At Montgisard, the boy king and his knights caught Saladin's army by surprise and broke it.
Saladin only escaped on a fast racing camel.
It took him years to rebuild his army.
He waited.

## Thirst at Hattin
A crusader lord named Reynald of Chatillon gave him his chance.
Reynald attacked Muslim caravans, and even sent raiding ships into the Red Sea, towards Mecca and Medina.
In 1187, in the middle of a truce, he attacked another caravan.
Saladin swore he would kill Reynald with his own hand.
He gathered the largest army he had ever led.
In July, he besieged the town of Tiberias, beside the Sea of Galilee.
The crusader army of King Guy of Jerusalem marched out to save it, across dry hills, in the summer heat.
Saladin's men blocked the way to the water.
His archers rode in close, shot, and rode away again.
Then they set the dry grass on fire, so the smoke blew into the faces of thirsty men in heavy armour.
On the fourth of July, on a hill with two peaks called the Horns of Hattin, the crusader army was destroyed.
King Guy and Reynald were brought to Saladin's tent.
Saladin handed the exhausted king a cup of iced water.
Guy drank, and passed the cup to Reynald.
Saladin made it clear that he had not given Reynald anything.
By custom, a man who is given a drink by his captor is under his protection.
Then he killed Reynald.
To the terrified king he said: kings do not kill kings.

## Jerusalem
After Hattin, the crusader kingdom had almost no soldiers left.
Town after town opened its gates.
In September 1187, Saladin reached Jerusalem.
Its defence was led by a nobleman named Balian of Ibelin.
He knighted every boy of noble birth he could find, just to put men on the walls.
When Saladin's engineers broke through the wall, Balian came out to talk.
He warned that if there were no terms, the defenders would fight to the last man and take the city down with them.
Saladin agreed to a surrender.
The people could leave, if they paid a ransom.
On the second of October 1187, Saladin entered Jerusalem.
Eighty-eight years earlier, the crusaders had filled these streets with blood.
This time, there was no massacre.
Thousands who could not pay were sold into slavery.
But thousands more were simply let go.
His brother asked for a thousand poor people as a gift, and set them all free.
Saladin released many of the old people and widows himself.
He did not destroy the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and later he let Christian pilgrims visit it.
He also invited Jews to come back and live in the city.
Even Christian writers noticed the difference.

## The Lionheart
The fall of Jerusalem shocked Europe.
The Pope called a new crusade, and three of the most powerful kings in Christendom answered.
The German emperor, Frederick Barbarossa, drowned crossing a river on the way, and most of his army went home.
King Philip of France came.
And so did King Richard of England, the man they called the Lionheart.
They met Saladin at the port of Acre.
For two years, the armies were locked around the city, in a war of hunger, disease and endless fighting.
In July 1191, Acre surrendered.
Talks about the prisoners dragged on.
In August, Richard lost his patience, and had about two thousand seven hundred Muslim prisoners killed, in sight of Saladin's army.
It was the darkest moment of the war.
Richard beat Saladin at Arsuf.
Twice he marched close enough to see the hills around Jerusalem.
And twice he turned back.
He knew that even if he took the city, he could never hold it once his army sailed home.

## A gift of horses
The two kings never met in person.
But they sent each other messages, and gifts.
In 1192, Saladin attacked the port of Jaffa.
Richard arrived by sea and jumped into the water to lead the counter-attack.
During the fighting, his horse was killed under him.
According to the chroniclers, Saladin's brother sent him two fine horses, so a king would not have to fight on foot.
When Richard fell ill with fever, Saladin is said to have sent him fruit, and snow from the mountains to cool his drinks.
Both armies were sick.
Both kings were exhausted.
In September 1192, they signed the Treaty of Jaffa.
The crusaders kept a strip of coast.
Jerusalem stayed with Saladin, but unarmed Christian pilgrims could visit freely.
Richard sailed home in October.
He never saw the Holy Land again.

## One gold coin
Saladin went back to Damascus, worn out by twenty years of war.
In February 1193 he caught a fever.
On the fourth of March, he died, aged about fifty-five.
His friend and biographer wrote that the city mourned him as no ruler had ever been mourned before.
All his life, Saladin had given money away, to his soldiers, to scholars, to the poor.
When his treasury was opened, it held one gold coin and forty-seven silver ones.
There was not enough to pay for his funeral.
The money had to be borrowed.
His empire did not last; his sons and brothers soon divided it.
But his name grew, and strangely, it grew most of all among his enemies.
European writers turned him into the perfect knight.
The Italian poet Dante placed him among the noble souls, beside the great heroes of ancient Greece and Rome.
In 1898, the German emperor paid for a new marble coffin for his tomb in Damascus.
It stands there to this day, empty.
Saladin still lies in his old wooden one.
