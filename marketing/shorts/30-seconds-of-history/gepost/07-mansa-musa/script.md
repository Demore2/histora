### Voice-over (clean, for TTS)
He gave away so much gold, its price in Cairo fell for years. So why did he go home in debt? This is Mansa Musa, king of Mali, a land of gold. In thirteen twenty-four, he crossed the Sahara to Mecca. His camels carried gold, and in Cairo, he gave it away to everyone. Twelve years later, gold there was still worth less. And on the way home, he ran out. The richest king of his age had to borrow money. That's thirty seconds of history.

## Fact notes
- **Pilgrimage 1324, through Cairo** (July 1324) — al-Umari, *Masalik al-Absar* (written in Cairo c. 1337-38, from people who met Musa); Ibn Khaldun.
- **Gold fell in value in Cairo and had not recovered twelve years later** — al-Umari: before Musa's visit the mithqal of gold "did not go below 25 dirhams"; afterwards "its value fell ... and it has remained cheap till now", not above 22 dirhams, "for about twelve years". That is a fall of roughly an eighth, **not a crash** — so the video says "fell" and "worth less", never "crashed the economy" (the popular version).
- **Gave gold to everyone** — al-Umari: he gave to the sultan's officials, the emirs, and lavishly to merchants and the people of Cairo.
- **Camels carried gold** — Ibn Khaldun reports loads of gold dust carried by the caravan; the famous numbers (60,000 people, 80-100 camels of gold) come from later, inflated retellings, so the video gives no number.
- **Ran out and borrowed on the way home** — al-Umari: his money ran short and he borrowed from Cairo merchants at high interest; the lender was later repaid (or not, depending on the account).
- **"The richest king of his age"** — the Catalan Atlas (1375) calls him "the richest and most noble lord of all this region, for the abundance of gold". The modern claim "the richest person who ever lived" can't be measured, so the video doesn't make it.
- **"A land of gold"** — Mali controlled or taxed the Bambuk and Bure goldfields — standard.
- **Map** — the route via Walata and Tuat is the standard trans-Saharan route, simplified to five stops; "Mali" sits at Niani, the capital, placed approximately (its exact site is disputed).
- **Image** — the Catalan Atlas detail (Abraham Cresques, 1375, BnF) is public domain; see `marketing/remotion/extra/BRONNEN.md`.
